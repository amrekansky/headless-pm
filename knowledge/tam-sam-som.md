# TAM / SAM / SOM

## What it is

TAM, SAM, and SOM are three concentric estimates that together answer "how big is this market opportunity?" TAM is the total universe, SAM is the portion you can realistically serve, and SOM is what you can actually capture in the near term. The framework is standard in investor pitches, strategic planning, and go-to-market design. Its value is not just the number — it's the logic behind the number, which forces you to articulate your customer, pricing, and competitive positioning.

- **TAM (Total Addressable Market)**: revenue if you captured 100% of the entire market with no competition
- **SAM (Serviceable Addressable Market)**: the portion of TAM reachable given your product's current capabilities, geography, and segment focus
- **SOM (Serviceable Obtainable Market)**: the portion of SAM you can realistically capture in 1-3 years given your resources, GTM, and competition

## When to use

- Investor fundraising (Series A+ requires billion-dollar TAM; pre-seed can work with less)
- Internal strategic planning: "is this bet worth our investment?"
- Go-to-market design: SOM forces you to pick a beachhead segment
- Evaluating whether to expand into a new market or product area
- Competitive narrative: "this market is too big for any one player to own"

## Core components / steps

### Top-Down vs Bottom-Up Approach

**Top-Down**
Start with a macro market size from an analyst report (Gartner, IDC, Statista, IBISWorld), then apply filters to get to SAM and SOM.

Example:
- Global HR software market: $30B (TAM) — from analyst report
- Mid-market companies in MENA + CIS with 50-500 employees: $600M (SAM) — applied geography + segment filter
- Realistically capturable in Year 1-2 with current resources: $15M (SOM) — applied GTM constraints

Weakness: "analyst report said $30B" is not credible unless you can show how you got from $30B to $600M with real logic.

**Bottom-Up**
Build the number from first principles: identify target customers, estimate how many exist, multiply by price.

Example:
- Target customer: mid-market ERP buyers in Kazakhstan, 200-1000 employees
- Estimated count: ~3,000 companies in this segment (from business registry data)
- Average contract value: $18,000/year
- SAM = 3,000 × $18,000 = $54M/year

Then estimate SOM: if you can close 2% in Year 1 → $1.08M ARR in Year 1.

**Why bottom-up wins with investors:** it shows you know your customer, your pricing, and your sales motion. You can defend every number. Top-down numbers look like you searched "market size" on Google.

**Best practice:** use both. Top-down as a sanity check on direction; bottom-up as the primary argument.

### Worked Example (B2B SaaS, PM tools)

**TAM:**
Global project/product management software market = ~$8B ARR (industry reports, 2024).

**SAM:**
Mid-market B2B SaaS companies (50-500 employees) in English-speaking markets needing PM tooling. Not the enterprise segment (too slow to sell, need big sales teams). Not solo freelancers (too price-sensitive). Estimate: 40% of TAM = $3.2B.

**SOM (Year 1-3):**
You're a 10-person company. Bottom-up: 500 target customers × $12,000 ACV = $6M ARR possible. Realistically at 20% win rate with current capacity: $1.2M ARR.

### Common Investor Objections

| Objection | Problem | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "Your TAM seems made up" | Top-down with no segmentation logic | Show bottom-up math, cite data sources |
| "That's a small market" | SAM too narrow | Show expansion path (adjacent segments, geographies) |
| "Your SOM is unrealistic" | No logic connecting SAM to SOM | Show conversion rates, sales capacity, comparable benchmarks |
| "This is a crowded market" | Didn't address competition in sizing | Explain why TAM is large enough for multiple winners, or your defensibility |

### How to Pressure-Test Estimates

1. **Denominator check**: where did the total count of potential customers come from? Is it verifiable?
2. **Price check**: is your ACV consistent with real deals in your pipeline, or hypothetical?
3. **Penetration check**: is your SOM % achievable? Compare to comparable companies at your stage.
4. **Competition check**: your SOM assumes you're competing for a real share — is the TAM growing, shrinking, or stable?
5. **Timing check**: is this a market that exists today or a market that will exist in 5 years? (Speculative TAMs are valid but need to be labeled.)

## Key questions to ask

- What data source supports the TAM number, and how recent is it?
- Have we calculated SAM bottom-up, or just filtered down top-down?
- What is our ACV based on — real deals, or assumptions?
- What does our SOM imply as a market penetration rate? Is that consistent with comparable companies?
- What's the TAM expansion story — what adjacent markets become accessible if we win this beachhead?

## Common mistakes

- **"The internet is our TAM"** — no segmentation, no credibility
- **Top-down only with no bottom-up validation** — "analyst report says $50B, we want 1% = $500M" is a cliche, not an argument
- **SOM bigger than realistic team capacity** — if you have 5 salespeople, your SOM implies a win capacity they can't execute
- **Confusing TAM with SAM** — your product can't serve every customer in the total market; acknowledge your constraints
- **Static market sizing** — don't show a single number; show growth rate and trajectory
- **Ignoring competitive reality in SOM** — the market exists but 3 incumbents have 80% share; your SOM math needs to account for this

## Quick reference

```
TAM = total universe (100% market share, no competition)
SAM = your reachable segment (product + geography + segment filters)
SOM = realistic capture (GTM capacity + competitive position + timeline)

Top-down: macro report → apply filters → sanity check
Bottom-up: count of customers × ACV → primary investor argument

Pressure test: verify denominator, price, penetration rate, and competition
```

**Investor expectation by stage:**
- Pre-seed: SAM > $50M, credible path
- Seed: SAM > $200M, some traction evidence
- Series A: TAM > $1B, SAM > $500M, proven unit economics
