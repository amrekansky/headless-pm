# Crossing the Chasm

## What it is

Geoffrey Moore's 1991 framework (updated in 2014) describes why most technology products fail to reach mainstream markets. The Technology Adoption Lifecycle has five segments: Innovators, Early Adopters, Early Majority, Late Majority, and Laggards. Between Early Adopters and Early Majority lies a dangerous gap — the Chasm — where most high-tech products stall and die. Crossing the Chasm is the playbook for bridging that gap.

## When to use

- Your product has early passionate users but growth has plateaued
- You're deciding which market segment to prioritize next
- Sales cycles are getting longer and win rates are falling
- You hear "we love the concept but we're not ready" from prospects
- Deciding whether to go broad (many segments) or deep (one beachhead)

## Core components / steps

### The Technology Adoption Lifecycle

```
Innovators → Early Adopters → [CHASM] → Early Majority → Late Majority → Laggards
    2.5%           13.5%                      34%              34%          16%
```

**Innovators (Techies)**: Love technology for its own sake. Buy unfinished products. Give free feedback. Small group, low revenue — but critical for credibility.

**Early Adopters (Visionaries)**: See strategic advantage in new technology. Willing to tolerate bugs and incomplete features. Drive deals on potential, not proof. Buy into the vision, not the product. Will fund your development as beta customers.

**Early Majority (Pragmatists)**: The mainstream. Want proven technology, references, clear ROI, vendor stability, and ecosystem support. Will NOT buy without seeing others like them succeed first. This is the Chasm.

**Late Majority (Conservatives)**: Only adopt when technology becomes standard. Price-sensitive. Want packaged solutions, not customization.

**Laggards**: Adopt only when forced.

### Why the Chasm exists

Early Adopters and Early Majority have fundamentally different buying criteria:

| Dimension | Visionary (Early Adopter) | Pragmatist (Early Majority) |
|-----------|--------------------------|----------------------------|
| Motivation | Competitive breakthrough | Productivity improvement |
| Risk tolerance | High — will fund R&D | Low — wants proven solutions |
| Reference check | Industry visionaries | Industry peers (like them) |
| Vendor relationship | Partners and co-builders | Established vendors |
| What they buy | Potential | Proof |

Visionaries can give you revenue but cannot give you Pragmatist references. Pragmatists only trust other Pragmatists.

### The Whole Product concept

Pragmatists don't buy core technology — they buy a complete solution. The Whole Product is everything required to make the technology work for them:
- Core product (generic)
- Expected product (minimum viable to fulfill the purchase promise)
- Augmented product (what differentiates you from alternatives)
- Potential product (what the product could become)

To cross the Chasm you must assemble the Whole Product for a narrow segment before expanding.

### Bowling Alley Strategy — choosing your beachhead

Moore's prescription: choose one specific, underserved niche in the Early Majority. Win it completely. Use that success as the "head pin" to knock over adjacent segments.

**Beachhead selection criteria:**
1. Urgent, unfunded pain that current solutions don't address
2. Small enough to win decisively with current resources
3. Clear economic buyer with budget authority
4. Referenceable — their success is visible to adjacent segments
5. Adjacent to larger segments you want to enter next

**Bowling pins**: Once the beachhead is won, identify the next segment that shares key characteristics. Each win enables the next.

### How PMs support Chasm crossing

1. **Package for the beachhead**: Stop building for everyone. Harden features the beachhead needs. Deliberately defer others.
2. **Assemble the Whole Product**: Build or partner for integrations, professional services, and support that Pragmatists require.
3. **Create a reference customer program**: One referenceable Pragmatist customer is worth ten Visionary case studies.
4. **Set positioning for the beachhead segment**: "For [segment] who have [problem], [product] is [category] that [differentiator]. Unlike [alternative], we [key advantage]."

## Key questions to ask

- Are we in the Chasm right now? (Signs: early enthusiasts love us; mainstream prospects stall or go dark.)
- Have we chosen a beachhead segment that is specific enough to win completely?
- Do we have the Whole Product assembled for that segment, or are we selling potential to Pragmatists?
- Do we have referenceable customers who look like our target Pragmatists?
- Are our sales reps selling Pragmatists using Visionary narratives?

## Common mistakes

- **Selling to too many segments simultaneously**: Each segment requires a different Whole Product. Spreading resources across five segments means you complete zero.
- **Using Visionary references with Pragmatist buyers**: "One of the biggest banks in Asia is using this" does not close a 300-person logistics company. They want to hear from companies like themselves.
- **Mistaking early revenue for Chasm crossing**: Strong early-adopter sales feel like momentum. They are not evidence that Pragmatists will follow.
- **Building for the future instead of the beachhead**: Pragmatists want "done" over "extensible." Shipping the integration they need beats architecting for a platform they don't care about.
- **No reference customer strategy**: Without deliberate cultivation of 3–5 referenceable wins in the beachhead, you cannot advance to the next bowling pin.

## Quick reference

| Stage | Buyer type | What they need from you |
|-------|-----------|------------------------|
| Pre-chasm | Innovators + Early Adopters | Vision, access, co-build |
| Crossing the Chasm | Early Majority (beachhead) | Whole Product, references, low risk |
| Bowling Alley | Adjacent Early Majority | Beachhead proof, packaged solution |
| Tornado | Broad Early Majority | Speed, standard, ecosystem |
