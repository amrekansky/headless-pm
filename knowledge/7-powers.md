# 7 Powers

## What it is

Hamilton Helmer's 7 Powers framework (from his 2016 book of the same name) defines the seven distinct sources of durable competitive advantage. Power is defined as a condition that creates persistent differential returns — meaning your business earns materially better margins than competitors in a way that resists competitive erosion. For PMs, understanding 7 Powers helps determine which product decisions build lasting moats vs. which ones are just short-term wins.

## When to use

- Evaluating whether a new feature or bet will create lasting advantage
- Competitive positioning conversations with leadership
- Deciding where to invest disproportionately on the roadmap
- Assessing a competitor's strategic position
- Defining what kind of company your product is actually building toward

## Core components / steps

### The 7 Powers

**1. Scale Economies**
Unit costs decrease as production volume grows. Fixed costs are spread over more units.
- Classic example: Netflix's content cost per subscriber falls as subscriber count rises.
- PM angle: Invest in infrastructure, tooling, and shared services that become more efficient with scale. Prioritize features that serve the largest segment of your user base.

**2. Network Economies (Network Effects)**
Value to each user increases as more users join. The product gets better as it gets bigger.
- Classic example: LinkedIn — the 70th million member gets more value than the 7th million member would have.
- PM angle: Identify whether you have same-side (direct) or cross-side (indirect) network effects. Prioritize growth loops over linear acquisition. Defensibility compounds.

**3. Counter-Positioning**
Your business model is so disruptive to the incumbent that if they copied you, it would hurt their own business.
- Classic example: Vanguard's index fund model threatened the high-margin active fund business of incumbents.
- PM angle: This power is often temporary and requires speed. If you have it, move before incumbents adapt. Useful in B2B when disrupting legacy software.

**4. Switching Costs**
Customers lose value if they switch to a competitor — due to data lock-in, workflow integration, or retraining cost.
- Classic example: Salesforce — migrating CRM data, workflows, and trained reps costs more than the alternative is worth.
- PM angle: Build deep integrations, custom configurations, and data export friction (ethically). High switching costs justify higher pricing. Track integration depth as a product metric.

**5. Branding**
Users pay a premium or prefer your product due to perceived trust, identity, or quality built over time.
- Classic example: Apple. Users pay 30–50% more for hardware that competes on spec with cheaper alternatives.
- PM angle: Branding takes years to build and can be lost in weeks. Product quality and consistent messaging compound into brand. Relevant for consumer products and B2B enterprise trust.

**6. Cornered Resource**
Privileged access to a scarce asset — data, talent, IP, or distribution — that competitors cannot replicate.
- Classic example: DeepMind's exclusive access to NHS patient data for training medical AI.
- PM angle: Identify proprietary data sets your product generates that competitors cannot get. First-party behavioral data, unique integrations, or licensed datasets can become cornered resources.

**7. Process Power**
Accumulated organizational capability — a way of doing things that is embedded, efficient, and impossible to copy quickly.
- Classic example: Toyota Production System. Competitors can read about lean manufacturing but cannot replicate 40 years of embedded culture.
- PM angle: Build into your product the institutional knowledge your team has accrued. Hard-to-copy internal processes (data labeling pipelines, review systems, QA rituals) compound into Process Power over years.

### Timing: when each power can be captured

| Phase | Powers available |
|-------|-----------------|
| Rapid growth | Scale Economies, Network Economies, Switching Costs |
| Post-growth (mature) | Branding, Process Power |
| Early (pre-PMF) | Counter-Positioning, Cornered Resource |

## Key questions to ask

- Which of the 7 powers does our product currently have, even in embryonic form?
- Is the advantage we're building one that compounds over time, or one a well-funded competitor can replicate in 18 months?
- Does this roadmap item strengthen an existing power or build a new one?
- Are we confusing a temporary competitive lead (first-mover) with actual power (first-mover advantage)?

## Common mistakes

- **Confusing features with moats**: A better UI is not a power. A competitor ships a better UI in 6 months. Data network effects are a power.
- **Assuming scale alone creates Power**: Size without a cost advantage mechanism is not Scale Economies. You need the math to show unit costs falling.
- **Ignoring timing**: Counter-Positioning erodes when incumbents adapt. Counter-Positioning is time-limited; build toward another power while the window is open.
- **Undervaluing Switching Costs**: PMs often optimize for onboarding ease but forget that depth of integration (that creates switching cost) is what retains enterprise customers long-term.

## Quick reference

| Power | Core mechanic | PM lever |
|-------|--------------|---------|
| Scale Economies | Cost per unit falls with volume | Invest in shared infrastructure |
| Network Economies | Value per user rises with user count | Build growth loops and cross-side value |
| Counter-Positioning | Copying would hurt the incumbent | Move fast while window is open |
| Switching Costs | Leaving costs more than staying | Deepen integrations, data portability |
| Branding | Premium willingness to pay | Consistent quality and messaging over time |
| Cornered Resource | Exclusive access to scarce asset | Own proprietary data and key talent |
| Process Power | Embedded organizational capability | Build institutional knowledge into product |
