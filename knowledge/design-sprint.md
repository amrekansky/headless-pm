# Design Sprint

## What it is

The Design Sprint is a 5-day structured process for answering critical product questions through design, prototyping, and testing with real users — without building anything production-ready. Created by Jake Knapp at Google, formalized at Google Ventures (GV), and published in the 2016 book "Sprint" (Knapp, Zeratsky, Kowitz). The core idea: compress months of debate and iteration into one focused week by building and testing a realistic prototype before committing to development.

The sprint delivers a validated answer (or a clear "this doesn't work") in 5 days with a team of 5-7 people.

## When to use

**Good fit:**
- High-stakes decision with significant uncertainty: "Should we build X? How should it work?"
- Team is stuck — lots of debate, no progress
- Major new feature, product, or pivot where failure is expensive
- Stakeholder alignment problem (everyone has a different vision)
- Need to test something before committing engineering resources

**Bad fit (when NOT to use):**
- Problem isn't clearly defined yet — sprint needs a specific challenge, not "figure out our strategy"
- The solution is already known and it's just execution
- No access to real target users for Friday testing (this is a hard requirement)
- Team can't commit 5 full consecutive days (interruptions kill sprints)
- The decision is low-stakes or easily reversible — sprint overhead isn't worth it

## Core components / steps

### Before the Sprint: Sprint Preparation

- Define the sprint challenge (specific, consequential question)
- Assemble the team: 5-7 people max
- Identify the **Decider** — the person with final authority (often a senior leader)
- Block 5 consecutive days; no laptops, no meetings
- Recruit 5 target users for Friday testing

### Day-by-Day Structure

**Monday — Understand**
- Map the problem space end-to-end
- Define the long-term goal and sprint questions
- Invite subject-matter experts to share knowledge in Lightning Talks (20 min each)
- Define the sprint target: one specific moment in the user journey to focus on

**Tuesday — Sketch (Diverge)**
- Each team member individually sketches solutions (4-step sketch process)
- No group brainstorming — individual thinking produces better ideas
- Output: a diverse set of detailed, annotated solution sketches

**Wednesday — Decide**
- Silent review and critique of all sketches
- Vote with dot stickers (everyone votes; Decider has extra votes)
- Decider makes the final call
- Create a storyboard (step-by-step prototype script, ~12-15 frames)

**Thursday — Prototype**
- Build a realistic-looking (not real) prototype based on the storyboard
- Typical tool: Keynote, Figma, or even paper — anything that looks credible to a user
- Assign roles: Maker, Writer, Asset Collector, Interviewer prep
- Target: prototype that fakes the experience convincingly enough for valid feedback

**Friday — Test (Validate)**
- 5 one-on-one user interviews, 60 minutes each
- Team watches via video feed in a separate room
- Observer takes notes on sticky notes (positive, negative, questions)
- Debrief: look for patterns across all 5 interviews
- Decision: what did we learn? What's the next step?

### The Decider Role

The Decider is not a rubber stamp. They participate in the sprint, observe user testing, and make the final call when the team is divided. Critically: the Decider's job is to **decide**, not to be the smartest person in the room. Jake Knapp emphasizes that without a clear Decider, sprints end in committee decisions.

### Sprint Questions

At the start, the team generates "sprint questions" — assumptions that could cause the product to fail:
- "Will users understand how to [key action]?"
- "Will users trust us enough to [share data / pay / etc.]?"
- "Does [core value proposition] resonate with [customer segment]?"

The prototype and test are designed to answer these specific questions.

## Key questions to ask

- Is this the right week to spend 5 days on this? (What's the cost of not knowing?)
- Who is the Decider, and are they committed to the full week?
- Can we get 5 target users in front of the prototype on Friday?
- What specific question must we answer by Friday?
- What would we do differently if the tests fail?

## Common mistakes

- **Wrong team composition** — no designers or no engineers; need both
- **No real Decider** — ends with "let's test both options" which dilutes the sprint
- **Recruiting wrong users for Friday** — the most common sprint failure; wrong users = wrong signal
- **Falling in love with the prototype** — it's a learning tool, not a deliverable
- **Skipping Monday mapping** — without shared understanding, Tuesday sketches diverge too broadly
- **Treating sprint result as a product decision** — 5 users give directional signal, not statistical proof
- **Running sprints on everything** — sprint fatigue; reserve for genuinely high-stakes unknowns

## Quick reference

```
Monday     → Understand: map the problem, pick target moment
Tuesday    → Diverge: individual sketching, many solutions
Wednesday  → Decide: vote, storyboard, one direction
Thursday   → Prototype: build a realistic fake
Friday     → Test: 5 users, look for patterns, decide next step
```

**The sprint doesn't end with a product. It ends with evidence.**

5 users won't be statistically significant, but 5 users will surface the patterns that matter. As Knapp notes: after 5 interviews, you'll see the same problems repeat — that's your signal.
