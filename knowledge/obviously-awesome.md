# Obviously Awesome: Product Positioning (April Dunford)

Positioning is the act of deliberately defining how your product is the best in the world at delivering value to a specific set of customers. Positioning is not a tagline, a pitch, or a marketing campaign — it's the context you set in the customer's mind before they even evaluate you. Get it wrong and your best features look mediocre; get it right and mediocre features look like strengths.

Source: April Dunford, *Obviously Awesome: How to Nail Product Positioning So Customers Get It, Buy It, Love It* (2019).

---

## The Core Problem: Stuck Positioning

Most companies inherit their positioning from the context in which they were founded. An early assumption ("we're building a database tool for developers") calcifies into positioning even after the product has evolved, the market has changed, and the best customers look nothing like the original target.

**Signs of stuck positioning:**
- Sales cycles are long because prospects don't immediately understand the value
- Customers use the product differently than the team intended — and love it more
- Win/loss analysis shows you're winning deals the team didn't expect and losing deals it thought were easy
- The same product is described differently by different salespeople
- Marketing generates MQLs that don't convert because the ICP doesn't match actual buyers

Stuck positioning is usually invisible from the inside — the team knows the product so well that the value is obvious to them. The fix is deliberate repositioning.

## The 5 Components of Positioning (in order)

The sequence matters. Each component builds on the previous one. Rushing to market category before understanding competitive alternatives produces positioning that feels right to the team but confuses customers.

### Component 1: Competitive Alternatives
**The question:** If your product didn't exist, what would customers use instead?

**Not competitors — alternatives.** This is the most common mistake. "Competitors" implies another company's product. Alternatives include:
- Another company's product (yes)
- A spreadsheet or homegrown tool
- A manual process / a person doing the work
- Doing nothing (status quo is always an option)
- A different category of product entirely

**Why it matters:** Customers evaluate your product against alternatives, not competitors. If they'd use a spreadsheet without you, you're not competing against Notion — you're competing against Excel. Your positioning must make you obviously better than a spreadsheet, not obviously better than Notion.

**How to find them:** Ask customers who bought what they were doing before, and what they considered in the purchase process. Sales teams know this — if they can name "the spreadsheet conversation" as a common objection, Excel is a real alternative.

### Component 2: Unique Attributes
**The question:** What does your product have or do that alternatives do not?

These are facts about your product — features, data, integrations, team expertise, delivery model, anything that is objectively true and not replicated by the alternatives you identified in Component 1.

**Rules:**
- Must be real (not aspirational) — if a competitor also has it, it's not unique
- Comparative to your specific alternatives (if your alternative is Excel, any automation is unique; if it's Salesforce, the bar is higher)
- Can be unexpected — a "limitation" (specialized for one industry, fewer features) can be a unique attribute if it creates value

### Component 3: Value
**The question:** What value do those unique attributes enable that alternatives cannot deliver?

This is the translation from features to customer outcomes. Attributes are facts; value is what those facts enable.

| Attribute | Value Enabled |
|-----------|---------------|
| Real-time collaboration | Teams can iterate without version conflicts, reducing revision cycles from days to hours |
| Single-tenant deployment | Compliance teams can certify it without a vendor risk review, eliminating the 6-month procurement process |
| No-code setup | A product manager can configure it without involving engineering, giving PMs control over their own workflow |

**Key insight:** Value is the bridge between what your product does and why a customer cares. "We have X feature" is an attribute; "so you can do Y faster/cheaper/better" is value. Customers buy value, not attributes.

### Component 4: Target Customers
**The question:** Who cares most about the value you deliver?

These are customers for whom your unique attributes deliver unusually high value — not "everyone who could benefit" but the segment for whom your strengths are the most valuable strengths.

**Characteristics of a well-defined target customer:**
- Has the problem acutely (not occasionally)
- Has tried alternatives and found them inadequate
- Can immediately see the value in your unique attributes
- Will be a reference customer and referral source
- Represents a segment large enough to build a business on

**The ICP trap:** Many teams define their ICP as "companies that can afford us and have our use case." That's a filter, not a target. A real target customer definition includes the characteristics that predict value realization — industry context, team structure, existing process, maturity of the problem.

### Component 5: Market Category
**The question:** In what market category does your product win?

The market category is the frame that sets customer expectations before they hear anything else about your product. Choose the wrong category and your best features look wrong; choose the right one and average features look strong.

**The "fastest horse" example:** A car positioned as "the fastest horse" will always lose because the evaluation criteria (speed, stamina, feeding) don't favor what makes a car valuable (fuel efficiency, cargo capacity, not needing to be fed). The car wins when it's in the "transportation" category and the evaluation criteria shift.

**Three positioning moves:**
1. **Head-to-head in existing market:** Declare yourself a better version of the existing category leader. Works when you're meaningfully better at what the market already values. Risk: you're evaluated by the incumbent's criteria.
2. **New segment in existing market:** Carve out a sub-niche ("enterprise version", "for [specific vertical]"). Works when a specific segment has needs the existing market doesn't serve. Risk: seen as a niche player.
3. **New market category creation:** Define a new category ("Product Analytics" vs "Web Analytics"). Works when your product delivers value in ways that don't fit existing categories. High risk, high reward — requires significant market education investment.

## Competitive Alternatives vs Competitors: A Critical Distinction

**Competitors:** Companies that sell a similar product and compete for the same deals.

**Competitive alternatives:** What customers actually do if your product disappears from their lives.

These are rarely the same. A team management tool's competitors (Asana, Monday.com) and its actual alternatives (email threads, spreadsheets, weekly standups) are completely different. Positioning against Asana requires proving you're better at task management. Positioning against spreadsheets requires proving that structure is worth the overhead.

Most positioning failure comes from writing copy to compete against named competitors while customers are actually comparing the product to a spreadsheet or status quo.

## Positioning Pitfalls

### "We have no competitors"
Every product has alternatives. "No competitors" means the team hasn't identified the real alternatives — usually because they're not looking at what customers actually do without the product. This statement is a red flag that kills sales conversations: if there are no competitors, why should I change at all?

### Feature-led positioning
Leading with attributes instead of value. "We have real-time collaboration, single-sign-on, and 200+ integrations" tells the customer what the product does, not why it matters to them. Attributes need to be translated to value before they resonate.

### "We're the best at X" without anchoring
Best compared to what? Without establishing the competitive alternatives first, "best" is meaningless. Best at what the customer cares about, compared to what they'd use instead, for which type of customer — all three anchors are required.

### Category confusion
Positioning in the wrong category forces the product to compete on criteria where it's weak. A specialized vertical tool positioned as a "general-purpose platform" will always lose to general-purpose platforms. Position in the category where your unique attributes are most valued.

---

## When to Reposition

Positioning should be revisited when:
- **ICP expands:** Early adopters are different from mainstream buyers; their jobs and values may differ
- **Competitive landscape shifts:** A major player enters or exits, changing what alternatives customers consider
- **Product evolves significantly:** New features have shifted what the product is genuinely best at
- **A new use case emerges:** Customers are using the product in ways that generate more value than the original use case
- **Win/loss data shows a pattern:** Consistently losing to unexpected competitors or winning in unexpected segments
