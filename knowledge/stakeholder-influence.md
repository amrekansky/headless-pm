# Stakeholder Influence — Frameworks for PMs Without Authority

## The PM's influence problem

Product managers rarely have direct authority over the people they depend on. Engineers, designers, sales reps, support leads, legal, finance, and executives all influence the product — and none of them report to the PM. This means every significant product decision requires influencing without authority.

This doc covers three complementary frameworks: SCARF (why people resist), Cialdini (what moves people), and difficult conversation structure (how to have the conversation without damaging the relationship).

## SCARF Model — Why Stakeholders Resist

Developed by David Rock (NeuroLeadership Institute). SCARF describes five social domains that the brain treats as threats or rewards, triggering the same fight-or-flight response as physical danger.

When a stakeholder resists your proposal, they're experiencing a threat in one of these dimensions:

| Domain | Threat Signal | PM Example |
|--------|--------------|------------|
| **Status** | "This diminishes my expertise or standing" | Questioning an engineer's technical estimate in public |
| **Certainty** | "I don't know what will happen if I say yes" | Asking for commitment to a feature with undefined scope |
| **Autonomy** | "This constrains my choices / I wasn't consulted" | Announcing a decision that affects their team without input |
| **Relatedness** | "I don't trust the person asking" | Asking a favor from someone you've never worked with directly |
| **Fairness** | "My team is carrying more than others for the same outcome" | Asking one team to take scope cuts others aren't taking |

**How to use SCARF:** Before any high-stakes ask, predict which dimension is at risk for your stakeholder. Design your approach to address that dimension first.

SCARF mitigations:
- **Status threat:** Acknowledge their expertise publicly. Ask for their opinion before making your case.
- **Certainty threat:** Provide a clear picture of what happens next. Offer a pilot or bounded commitment.
- **Autonomy threat:** Give them a real choice. Involve them in shaping the solution before the ask.
- **Relatedness threat:** Build the relationship before you need it. Have low-stakes interactions first.
- **Fairness threat:** Acknowledge the imbalance. Offer visible credit or future reciprocity.

## Cialdini's Six Principles — What Moves People

From Robert Cialdini's "Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion." Applied to PM cross-functional context:

**Reciprocity:** People feel obligated to return favors. Before a big ask, invest in the other person. Write a recognition note to their skip-level. Volunteer for something that helps their team. Credit them publicly.

**Commitment:** People want to be consistent with past statements. Get a small yes first. "Would you be open to looking at the data together?" is easier to say yes to than "Will you approve this?" A small yes sets up the bigger yes through the principle of consistency.

**Social proof:** People look to others' behavior for guidance. Name who has already said yes. "Engineering and design are aligned — I'm coming to you as the last piece." Social proof is most powerful when the others are peers or respected figures.

**Authority:** People follow credible experts. Bring in a data point, an external consultant's view, or a senior PM's perspective to support your case. Make your own authority credible with clear reasoning, not just title.

**Liking:** People say yes to people they like. Build genuine personal rapport before high-stakes asks. Learn what matters to your stakeholder outside work. Find common ground.

**Scarcity:** People act when they sense a closing window. Be honest about real deadlines and real constraints. Artificial scarcity backfires — use it only when the constraint is real.

## Difficult Conversation Structure

From "Difficult Conversations" (Stone, Patton, Heen, Harvard Negotiation Project). Three conversations happen simultaneously when stakes are high:

1. **The "What Happened" conversation** — Who's right? What should be done? (Surface level, where most people get stuck)
2. **The feelings conversation** — What emotions are each person experiencing? (Often unspoken but driving behavior)
3. **The identity conversation** — What does this mean about me? (The deepest layer; most resistant to reason)

**Structure for a stakeholder alignment conversation:**

Step 1 — Name your intent explicitly: "I want to find something that works for both of us, not convince you I'm right."

Step 2 — Describe the impact, not the behavior: Not "You keep changing priorities," but "When priorities shift weekly, the team loses context and misses commitments."

Step 3 — Invite their perspective before making your ask: "I know you're seeing this differently — help me understand what's driving that for you."

Step 4 — Separate position from interest: Their position: "I don't want to delay launch." Their interest: "I need the sales team to have something to sell." Address the interest, not the position.

Step 5 — Offer a joint problem-framing: "What if we figured out together what the minimum viable launch looks like that gives sales enough to work with?"

## Common PM influence mistakes

**Leading with the solution.** Stakeholders who weren't involved in the problem framing resist solutions. Frame the conversation as joint problem-solving, not solution advocacy.

**Addressing the stated objection instead of the SCARF trigger.** When someone says "the timeline is too aggressive," they often mean "I'm afraid of what happens if my team fails this." Addressing the timeline (Certainty) may miss the real issue (Fairness or Status).

**Building the relationship only when you need something.** Influence capital is built before you need it. PMs who only engage stakeholders at decision points start every ask at a deficit.

**Treating resistance as irrational.** Every stakeholder resistance makes sense from their perspective. Your job is to understand that perspective, not to overcome it.

## Quick reference

**SCARF — 5 resistance dimensions:** Status (expertise threatened) / Certainty (unknown outcome) / Autonomy (no choice) / Relatedness (no trust) / Fairness (unequal burden). Identify which is active before your ask.

**Cialdini — 6 influence levers:**
- Reciprocity: invest before asking
- Commitment: get a small yes first
- Social proof: name who already agreed
- Authority: bring data or a respected voice
- Liking: build rapport before high stakes
- Scarcity: use only when the constraint is real

**Difficult conversation steps:** (1) State intent — finding what works, not winning. (2) Describe impact, not behavior. (3) Ask their perspective before presenting yours. (4) Address their interest (why), not their position (what). (5) Offer joint problem-framing.
