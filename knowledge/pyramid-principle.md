# Pyramid Principle

## What it is

The Minto Pyramid Principle is a communication framework developed by Barbara Minto at McKinsey & Company in the 1970s. Instead of building toward a conclusion, you lead with it — then support it with logically grouped evidence. This "answer-first" approach dramatically reduces cognitive load for busy executives and increases decision speed.

## When to use

- Writing executive emails, memos, or status updates
- Structuring PRDs, strategy docs, and product briefs
- Preparing slide decks for leadership reviews
- Any communication where the reader has limited time and needs to act

## Core components / steps

### BLUF — Bottom Line Up Front

The first sentence or slide states your recommendation, finding, or ask. Do not build suspense. Executives read the first sentence, then decide whether to keep reading. If your conclusion is buried on slide 12, they will never reach it.

### MECE — Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive

Minto coined MECE (pronounced "meece") as the governing principle for grouping supporting ideas:
- **Mutually exclusive**: no overlap between buckets (e.g., "revenue" and "sales revenue" are not ME)
- **Collectively exhaustive**: all buckets together cover the complete picture with no gaps

MECE prevents double-counting, ensures nothing is forgotten, and makes your logic auditable.

### SCR — Situation, Complication, Resolution

The standard narrative arc for executive communication:
1. **Situation**: what is true and agreed upon right now (shared context)
2. **Complication**: what changed, what problem exists, what tension is created
3. **Resolution**: your recommended action or answer

Example:
- Situation: "We launched the feature in Q3 to three enterprise accounts."
- Complication: "Activation rate is 12% — 60 points below forecast — and two accounts are at risk of churn."
- Resolution: "I recommend pausing acquisition and running a 3-week activation sprint before the next sales cycle."

### Pyramid structure

```
[Main Point / BLUF]
├── Key Argument 1
│   ├── Supporting data
│   └── Supporting data
├── Key Argument 2
│   ├── Supporting data
│   └── Supporting data
└── Key Argument 3
    ├── Supporting data
    └── Supporting data
```

Supporting arguments must be MECE. Each level answers "why?" or "so what?" about the level above.

## Key questions to ask

- What is the one thing I need the reader to do or believe after reading this?
- Is my opening sentence the conclusion, or am I still warming up?
- Are my three to five key arguments mutually exclusive?
- Do the key arguments together cover 100% of the case (collectively exhaustive)?
- Does each supporting data point directly prove the argument above it?

## Common mistakes

- **Burying the lead**: Starting with context and history, leaving the recommendation for last. Executives skim — they need the point in sentence one.
- **Non-MECE groupings**: "Revenue is down" and "enterprise revenue is down" are not two separate points. Collapsing these is the fix.
- **Too many levels**: More than three tiers of hierarchy usually signals unclear thinking, not thoroughness.
- **SCR in the wrong order**: Spending too long on Situation before naming the Complication kills momentum.
- **Weak resolution**: Ending with "we should consider options" instead of a clear recommendation. Executives want a decision framed, not deferred.

## Quick reference

| Element | Purpose | One-liner test |
|---------|---------|----------------|
| BLUF | Lead with conclusion | "Can I cut everything before my recommendation and still make sense?" |
| MECE | Structure arguments | "Do my buckets overlap? Do they cover everything?" |
| SCR | Narrative arc | "Situation accepted → Complication creates tension → Resolution resolves it?" |
| Pyramid | Document structure | "Does each lower level answer 'why is that true?' about the level above?" |

**Rule of thumb**: If a first-time reader can understand your main point in 10 seconds, your communication is structured correctly.
